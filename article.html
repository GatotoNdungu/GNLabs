Tuning a PID loop against a model before it ever sees a real tank

GN

GN Labs

Jul 19, 2026 · 9 min read
Share
← All articles

Most PID loops in the field were tuned live, on the real process, by nudging gains until the overshoot looked tolerable. That works, right up until "tolerable" costs you a batch. Here's the alternative: build a model of the thing you're heating first, tune against that, and only then let the tuned gains anywhere near a tank that actually matters.

The project that forced this discipline on me was a four-tank batch heater retrofit, the same one described in the relay logic write-up — a client with a mixing line where thermal overshoot during heat-up was scorching product on the tank walls before the loop caught up. Nobody wanted a repeat of that during commissioning, so the tuning had to be right on the first real attempt, not the fifth.

Why model first instead of tune live

Live tuning is popular because it's fast and it doesn't require building anything extra. It also means every experiment you run — every step change, every aggressive gain you try — happens on equipment that's actually making product. If an integral term winds up and overshoots by 15°C, that's not a chart on a screen, that's a batch.

A model turns tuning into something you can be reckless with. You can push a PID loop into oscillation, watch it fall apart, and learn exactly why — with zero consequence beyond CPU time. The only real cost is the week or two it takes to build a model good enough to trust, which is almost always cheaper than one ruined batch.

If you can't afford to be wrong on the real system, you can't afford to skip the model. — a lesson learned the expensive way, twice

Building a thermal model worth trusting

The tank in question was a jacketed, 800-liter vessel with steam-heated glycol in the jacket. A first-principles lumped model gets you most of the way: treat the product and the jacket as two thermal masses coupled by a heat-transfer coefficient, and drive the jacket temperature from steam valve position.

The two-mass model earns its keep because it captures something a simple first-order approximation misses entirely: the jacket heats up faster than the product, so there's a real lag between valve action and product temperature response. That lag is exactly what makes naive tuning dangerous — a gain that looks sluggish in the first ten seconds can still be building toward a large overshoot a minute later.

Fitting parameters from real data

Rather than trust datasheet values for the heat-transfer coefficient, we ran three open-loop step tests on the actual tank — steam valve stepped from 20% to 60%, response logged at 1 Hz — and fit the two time constants and the gain against that data in scipy.optimize.curve_fit. Twenty minutes of real data produced a model that matched the plant's step response within about 4%, which was more than good enough for tuning purposes.

thermal_model.py # Two-mass jacket/product model, discretized at dt = 1s def step(state, valve_pct, dt=1.0): T_jacket, T_product = state # Jacket responds fast to valve position dTj = (K_STEAM * valve_pct - T_jacket) / TAU_JACKET # Product lags the jacket through the wall coefficient dTp = (T_jacket - T_product) / TAU_PRODUCT T_jacket += dTj * dt T_product += dTp * dt return (T_jacket, T_product)

Field note

If you only have time for one open-loop test, run it at the amplitude closest to your actual operating step. Model fits degrade fastest at the edges of the range you tested — a model tuned on a 20→60% step will misbehave more than you'd expect near 90%.

Tuning against the model

With a model that behaved like the real tank, tuning became a search problem instead of a live experiment. We ran a grid sweep of Kp, Ki, and Kd against a standard setpoint step, scoring each combination on overshoot, settling time, and integral of absolute error. Three candidate gain sets came out ahead of the rest:

Gain setOvershootSettling timeNotes
Conservative0.8°C340sSafe default; a little slow for high-throughput days
Balanced2.1°C210sChosen for production
Aggressive5.6°C150sFaster, but eats into the scorch margin

We picked "Balanced" — not the fastest option, but the one with the largest margin between its overshoot and the temperature where product actually starts to scorch. Optimizing purely for settling time is a classic trap: the model will happily hand you gains that are fast right up until a slightly fouled heat exchanger or a colder-than-usual feed pushes the real system past a threshold the simulation never saw.

  1. Fit the model against open-loop step data from the real tank, not datasheet values.
  2. Sweep gains against the model and score on overshoot, settling time, and IAE together — never on speed alone.
  3. Pick the gain set with margin against your actual failure threshold, not the one that wins the benchmark.
  4. Validate the chosen gains on a hardware-in-the-loop rig before they ever reach the PLC controlling the real tank.
"Fastest settling time" is a benchmark score. "Largest margin before the batch scorches" is an engineering decision. A model will happily give you the first one if you don't ask for the second.

Validating on hardware-in-the-loop

A model, however well fitted, is still a simplification — which is why the gains never went straight from simulation to the plant. Between the two sat a hardware-in-the-loop bench: the actual PLC and actual steam valve actuator, wired to a small real-time simulator running the same thermal model in place of the physical tank. This is roughly the same rig described in the HIL bench article, repurposed for a heating loop instead of a mixing sequence.

Running the real controller hardware against a simulated plant catches a category of bug that a pure software simulation cannot: PLC scan-time jitter, valve actuator deadband, and the very real fact that the steam valve doesn't move instantly no matter what the model assumes. On this project, HIL testing surfaced a 400ms actuator lag that wasn't in the original model at all — small enough to ignore for slow tuning, large enough to matter once we tightened the loop.

What actually shipped

The "Balanced" gain set, adjusted slightly after HIL testing exposed the actuator lag, went into production on all four tanks. Batch variance — the figure that started this whole project — dropped from roughly 6% to under 1%,1 and in eight months of operation there hasn't been a single scorch event traceable to overshoot.

None of this required exotic control theory. A two-mass model, a grid search, and a HIL bench are all things most controls teams already have access to — the discipline is in refusing to skip straight from "the model looks good" to "ship it," and in scoring gain candidates on margin rather than speed.

  1. Measured across 40 consecutive batches pre- and post-retrofit, same product and ambient conditions. ↩
